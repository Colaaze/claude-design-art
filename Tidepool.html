What a tide pool is

When the sea retreats, it leaves water trapped in hollows of the rock. Each pool is a small, cut-off piece of ocean that has to keep its residents alive until the tide returns.

How this site is arranged

The navigation runs down the shore in the order the falling tide uncovers it: the spray, the high, middle and low pools, the kelp edge, and then the returning sea. The gauge shows height above the lowest tide. Its figures are illustrative.

Made to be touched

The water answers your pointer. Move across it for small ripples, or click and tap for a larger one. The slow button on the last page calms it.

the spray

Above the high-tide line  ·  wet only by spray

This is the highest band of the shore, above where the tide normally reaches. Water arrives here only as spray and storm waves, so the rock is dry for most of the day. It is the edge where sea life gives way to land.

The rock is stained black and orange by lichens, and the only animals are the ones that can put up with salt, sun and long hours without any moisture.

Periwinkles

Small snails that wedge into cracks and seal their shells with a lid to hold in moisture. Some graze this zone and can go for days without being wet.

Lichen bands

The black and orange stains on the rock are lichens and tiny algae. They can dry out completely and revive the moment they are wet again.

Rock lice

Flat, grey and quick, these relatives of pill bugs hide in cracks by day and come out at night to feed on algae and dead material.

high pools

+2 to +3 m  ·  reached only by the highest tides

Pools this high are refilled by the sea only around the biggest tides. The rest of the time they depend on rain and spray, and each one behaves like a small experiment in extremes.

On a hot day the water warms by several degrees and grows saltier as it evaporates. After rain it can turn almost fresh. Only the toughest residents stay.

Pools that swing

Temperature and saltiness can change a great deal within a single day. A resident here has to cope with all of it, with no way to leave.

Tiny copepods

Red-orange specks darting through the water are copepods, small crustaceans that tolerate extreme swings better than almost any animal on the shore.

Sea lettuce

Thin green sheets of algae grow quickly where light is strong, and they survive the salt swings that kill most of their neighbors.

middle pools

+1 to +2 m  ·  covered about half of each day

Here the sea comes and goes every few hours, so the shore is half land and half ocean. The pools are deeper, the water stays cooler, and the crowd thickens.

Mussels, barnacles and anemones cover almost every surface. The main story is competition for space: the best rock is taken, and everything else is packed in around it.

Anemones

At low tide they close into soft knobs. In the pool they open and wait for food to touch their stinging tentacles. Some clone themselves into carpets of identical animals.

Hermit crabs

They have no shell of their own. As they grow they trade up, and crabs will sometimes queue by size to pass shells along a chain.

Mussels

Anchored by tough threads, mussels hold the most space on the mid shore, and they give shelter to many smaller animals living among them.

low pools

0 to +1 m  ·  exposed only at the lowest tides

These pools sit close to the edge of the sea and are uncovered only briefly. Their water stays cool and clear, which makes them the richest and most colorful on the shore.

Animals that cannot stand long drying live here: sea stars, urchins and the pink crusts of coralline algae.

Sea stars

Predatory sea stars feed on mussels and keep them from taking over. Removing them from a shore changes it, which is why ecologists call them keystone predators.

Urchins

Purple urchins graze algae, and in some places they scrape the rock into shallow bowls where they sit sheltered from waves.

Sculpins

These small, rock-colored fish live in the pools and are known to find their way home to the same pool after being moved some distance away.

the kelp edge

Below 0 m  ·  underwater except at the very lowest tides

Past the last pools, the rock slopes into water that almost never leaves. Giant seaweeds called kelp anchor themselves with root-like holdfasts and rise toward the light.

The pools above get much of their food and many of their young from here. Larvae and drifting scraps ride the tide up the shore.

Holdfasts

Kelp has no true roots. A tangle called a holdfast grips the rock, and tiny animals live inside it like a hidden village.

Fast growth

Giant kelp can grow as much as half a meter in a single day, forming a canopy that shelters fish below.

A nursery

Many pool animals release larvae into the open water. Kelp forests give young fish somewhere to hide before they move on.

the returning tide

The sea comes back

Then the tide turns. Water spills over the low pools first, then the middle ones, then the high ones, one basin at a time, until the shore is under the sea again.

Everything that closed up opens. Anemones unfurl, barnacles extend feathery legs to filter food, and the animals that were waiting begin to feed.

Why the sea moves

The Moon’s gravity pulls the ocean into bulges, and the Earth turns beneath them. Most coasts see two highs and two lows a day, about 25 minutes later each day.

Spring and neap

Tides are strongest around the new and full moon and weakest at the quarter moons. The highest pools are filled only at the strongest tides.

Glowing water

On some warm nights, waves glow blue where tiny plankton called dinoflagellates are abundant. It is a light show that only appears with the sea.
